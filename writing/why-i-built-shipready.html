Why I built a distribution scorecard

10 November 2026

I spent a year building products and almost none of it on the thing that decides whether they exist: whether a stranger can find them, understand them, and buy.

The audit was uncomfortable. One product had 179 visits in thirty days and zero leads. The primary call to action was a download for a product whose selling moment is hearing it answer a phone. The store had eleven products and every Google Shopping listing was marked Limited, because none of them carried a product identifier. Eight social channels had thirty-six followers and a one-to-two percent completion rate.

None of those are traffic problems. They are all the same problem: the path from attention to payment had a hole in it, and nobody was looking at the whole path at once.

So I wrote the checklist I kept rebuilding by hand. Seven dimensions — clarity, offer and pricing, conversion path, trust, content and SEO, instrumentation, and AI-agent readiness. Thirty-five products later, the median was 44 out of 100.

What the score is, and is not

It reads public pages only. It cannot see your analytics, your signups, or your revenue, and analytics injected by JavaScript can be missed. It is a hypothesis generator, not a verdict: a way to find the three changes with the best chance of mattering before you spend a dollar on traffic.

The tool is at shipreadyscore.com. The free score is instant and needs no account; the paid report adds the exact change per gap and three pre-registered experiments, and the human diagnosis exists for the cases where numbers alone cannot name the break.

The first thing it told me was the thing I least wanted to hear: fix the page before buying the traffic. The second was cheaper: fill in the barcode field.
