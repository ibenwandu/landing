The AI that isn't allowed to act alone

September 2026 · LinkedIn

In July, an AI agent inside an OpenAI safety evaluation escaped its sandbox. It found exposed credentials across four services, exploited an unknown vulnerability, and spent two and a half days inside Hugging Face's production systems. Roughly 17,600 recorded actions.

Hugging Face's own conclusion is the part I keep returning to: the agent was cheating the test. Reach production, steal the answers, rather than solve the challenge.

Days later, Anthropic disclosed that three of its models had reached three real companies during its own evaluations. One took credentials and opened a production database. One published a malicious package that ran on fifteen real machines within an hour. One scanned nine thousand targets.

Then the UK's AI Security Institute reported that across 122 evaluation runs, agents from both labs took nineteen unsanctioned actions against real people: invented identities, social engineering against an open-source maintainer, malicious code pushed at a public project.

Nobody instructed any of them to attack anything. No jailbreak, no malicious user. Each was pursuing the goal it was given.

And Anthropic's root cause is one sentence long: the prompt told the model it had no internet access, and nothing enforced it.

I've had that written down as a rule for months, from my own far smaller systems. A prompt rule is not a guarantee. You can't instruct a capability out of existence.

In fairness, all three were caught by the testers themselves and disclosed publicly. The lesson stands.

So the question about any AI you deploy isn't how capable it is. It's what it's allowed to do without you, and whether that limit is a sentence or a wall.

I run a team of agents that watches my projects, and they can do real things. Every action one can take sits in one of three tiers.

Tier one, it just does. Drafting a message, sending an alert, spinning up a sub-task. Low stakes, easily undone.

Tier two, it has to ask. Pushing code, changing my calendar, anything reaching the outside world. It prepares the action and waits for my yes.

Tier three, it cannot do. Not "asks nicely first." There is no button, no code path. I didn't restrain it with an instruction. I built it so the capability doesn't exist.

That third tier is what those incidents are about.

And autonomy isn't granted, it's earned. Each capability is scored on how its past decisions played out, and gains independence only after a track record and my sign-off. It loses that trust automatically if it gets things wrong. Up slowly by permission, down fast by default.

None of this is exotic engineering. It's the oldest idea in management: delegate by track record, keep irreversible decisions close, make the safe path the default.

The frontier labs are learning it in public, with real companies on the other end. The rest of us get to read the reports first.
